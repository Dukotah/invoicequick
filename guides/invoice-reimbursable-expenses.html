How to Invoice for Reimbursable Expenses

When you spend money out-of-pocket on behalf of a client — a train ticket to their office, a stock photo license for their landing page, materials for their shoot — you need to get that money back. This guide explains which expenses qualify, how to document them properly, and exactly how to add them to an invoice so payment is fast and disputes are rare.

What counts as a reimbursable expense

A reimbursable expense is an out-of-pocket cost you incur specifically for a client project that the client agreed in advance to cover. The two tests are: (1) you actually paid for it; and (2) the client agreed to reimburse it before you spent the money.

Reimbursable — bill it

Project-specific costs

Travel and accommodation to the client's site • Materials bought for their deliverable • Software licenses purchased for their project • Third-party services commissioned on their behalf • Permits or filing fees required for their work • Equipment rented for their shoot or event

Not reimbursable — your overhead

General business costs

Your own computer and hardware • Your internet connection • Software you use across all clients (Adobe CC, Notion, Figma) • Your home office • Professional development • General business insurance

Overhead is already priced into your hourly or project rate. Billing it again as an expense is a common source of client friction and sometimes disputes. If your rate genuinely doesn't cover a tool you bought solely for a client's project, the right fix is to quote it as a line item in the project estimate, not to add it unexpectedly at invoice time.

The contract foundation: get it in writing first

Expenses are only billable if your contract says they are. The minimum clause to include:

Client will reimburse Designer for all pre-approved, project-related
out-of-pocket expenses, billed at cost with supporting receipts.
Expenses exceeding $[threshold] require written approval before they
are incurred.

Without this language, a client can dispute every expense line item on the grounds that they never agreed to pay it. Even a one-line email that says "yes, go ahead and book a train ticket, we'll cover it" is better than nothing — forward it to yourself as a dated record.

Never surprise a client with expenses at invoice time. If your contract says "plus expenses" but you haven't mentioned any costs during the project, a large expense line on the final invoice is likely to stall payment. Send a quick note when you incur a significant cost: "Just a heads-up — I bought the stock image license today, $129, will appear on the invoice."

Pre-approval: the one habit that eliminates disputes

The single most effective practice for smooth expense reimbursement is getting written approval before spending. Set a threshold in your contract — typically $50 to $100 for small projects, $200+ for larger engagements — above which you message the client before spending.

  • State the expense, the amount, and why it's needed: "I need to buy a Mapbox API key for the interactive map, one-time cost $120 — OK to proceed?"
  • Keep the approval (email, Slack, text) linked to the expense receipt in your records
  • For travel, get approval for the specific trip before booking — not just a general "travel may be needed"

Under-$50 consumables and nominal costs (parking, postage, a print for review) are generally fine to bill without prior approval if your contract covers expenses broadly, but flag them in your project notes so they're no surprise on the invoice.

Common reimbursable expense categories

Category Examples Notes
Travel Train, plane, bus, taxi, ride-share, mileage IRS mileage rate (US): 67¢/mile for 2024; keep a mileage log. For air travel, economy-class only unless contract specifies otherwise.
Accommodation Hotel, short-term rental for overnight trips Reasonable rates only; a client can push back on a luxury hotel if the contract says "reasonable expenses".
Meals Meals during travel away from home base, client entertainment with the client present Personal meals at home or while commuting locally are overhead. Many clients cap meals at a daily per-diem; ask upfront.
Materials & supplies Print materials, packaging, fabric, props, hardware for their project Keep itemised receipts. Bulk materials bought partly for the project: prorate or quote ahead.
Software & assets Stock photo/video licenses, fonts, WordPress plugins, one-time API keys bought for the project If you retain the license for reuse, it may be a shared cost — pro-rate or negotiate ownership in the contract.
Third-party services Copywriting, photography, translation, printing, couriers Subcontractor fees are typically billed at cost or with a 10–15% management markup if you're coordinating them.
Permits & fees Location permits, filing fees, government fees for their business Bill at exact cost with receipt; no markup is typically expected here.

Markup: pass-through vs handling fee

You have two options:

  • Pass-through (at cost): Bill the exact amount you paid. Standard for most freelance work and easiest to justify. The client sees the receipt matches the invoice line item.
  • Handling markup (10–15%): Common in agency and production work where you're coordinating multiple vendors, managing supplier relationships, and carrying financial risk on behalf of the client. Must be in your contract.

If your contract is silent on markup, bill at cost. Adding an undisclosed markup is a quick way to lose client trust and trigger disputes. If you believe your coordination work deserves a handling fee, write it into your next engagement agreement rather than applying it retroactively.

How to add expenses to your invoice

Add each expense as its own line item, separate from your labor fee. Be specific — a vague "Expenses — $340" will get questioned. The description should answer three things: what was it, when was it, and why was it for this project.

Ready-to-paste line item descriptions:

Travel — round-trip train, [City A] to [City B], 14 Nov 2026           $87.00
Accommodation — 1 night hotel, client on-site visit, 14–15 Nov 2026   $149.00
Stock image licenses — Shutterstock ×3, homepage hero (inv #SI-1104)   $79.00
Typeface license — [Font name], perpetual desktop+web (receipt attch.) $129.00
Subcontractor copywriting — homepage + 3 product pages, Oct 2026       $600.00
  (Handling fee 10%)                                                     $60.00
Materials — foam core, adhesive, cutting mat for prototype mockup       $34.50
Mileage — client site visit, 42 mi × $0.67 (IRS 2024 rate)             $28.14
Include the receipt reference in the line item description, not just as an attachment. "Invoice #SI-1104" or "receipt attached" tells the client's bookkeeper exactly where to find the backup documentation without opening every attachment.

Receipts and documentation

Keep every receipt, even for small amounts. Best practice:

  • Photograph or scan receipts the same day — paper fades fast
  • Name files consistently: 2026-11-14-hotel-clientname.pdf
  • Attach receipts to the invoice email, or offer them on request if the client prefers a leaner email
  • For mileage, keep a simple log: date, start/end point, purpose, miles — a spreadsheet works fine
  • Retain records for at least 3 years (7 years for US tax purposes)

Some clients, especially corporate ones, have an expense report process and will send you their own form to fill in. Ask at project kick-off whether they have a preferred format so you're not reformatting everything after the project ends.

Same invoice or separate expense invoice?

Both work. The right choice depends on project complexity and client preference.

ApproachBest forWatch out for
Add to the main invoice Small number of expenses; single invoice per milestone A long list of expense lines can obscure the labor fee — subtotal each section
Separate expense invoice Large projects with ongoing expenses; clients with separate AP workflows for expenses Two invoices = two approval steps; flag this at the outset so the client isn't surprised

If you use a separate expense invoice, number it consistently with your main invoices (e.g. INV-2026-047-EXP) and reference the project name on both so the client can match them easily.

Tax note (US): Expense reimbursements under a qualifying accountable plan — where you document the business purpose, provide receipts, and return any excess — are generally not taxable income for you. Reimbursements outside those conditions may be treated as income. This guide is not tax advice; consult a tax professional for your situation.

Frequently asked questions

What are reimbursable expenses on an invoice?

Reimbursable expenses are out-of-pocket costs you incur specifically for a client's project that the client agreed in advance to cover. Examples include travel, accommodation, materials, stock assets, and third-party software licenses bought for that project. They are listed as separate line items on the invoice, distinct from your labor fee.

Can I charge a markup on reimbursable expenses?

Yes, if your contract says so. A 10–15% handling fee is common in agency work to cover the time and risk of managing the spend. Many freelancers pass expenses through at cost with no markup. Whichever you choose, state it in your contract before work starts — adding a markup to an invoice that doesn't mention it will cause disputes.

Do I need receipts to invoice for expenses?

Always keep every receipt. Many clients require copies with the invoice; some ask for an expense report attachment. For US freelancers, an IRS-compliant accountable plan requires receipts or other documentation for each expense. Even if a client doesn't ask, receipts are your protection if the reimbursement is ever questioned.

Should I put expenses on the same invoice as my labor fee?

Either is acceptable. For small, infrequent expenses, adding them as separate line items on the same invoice is simplest. For larger or complex projects with ongoing expense tracking, a separate expense report invoice keeps billing cleaner and makes it easier for the client's accounts payable team to process. Ask your client which they prefer at the start of the engagement.

What is the difference between reimbursable and non-reimbursable expenses?

Reimbursable expenses are project-specific, out-of-pocket costs the client agreed to cover — travel to their site, materials bought for their project, a stock image license for their deliverable. Non-reimbursable expenses are your general business overhead: your own computer, your internet connection, software subscriptions you use across all clients, your home office. Overhead is already priced into your rate and cannot be billed separately.

How do I word expense line items on an invoice?

Be specific: include the expense category, the date, and what it was for. For example: “Travel — round-trip train to client site, 14 Nov 2026” or “Stock image license — Shutterstock, 3 images for homepage hero (invoice #SI-2026-1104)”. Vague entries like “Expenses — $340” invite disputes and make the client's bookkeeper's life harder.

Are reimbursed expenses taxable income for freelancers?

In the US, reimbursements under a qualifying accountable plan — where you document the business purpose, provide receipts, and return any excess — are generally not taxable income. If reimbursements don't meet those conditions they may be treated as income. Rules vary in other countries. Consult a tax professional for your specific situation; this guide is not tax advice.
